This note defines a finite probability model for independently and uniformly
sampled identifiers and proves its collision bound. For at most one billion
256-bit samples, the probability of any repeated identifier is below $2^{-128}$.
The proof covers sampling outcomes and their conversion to participant IDs;
the node runtime still accepts manually supplied natural numbers and has no
random ID generator. Randomness assumptions follow the distinction between
output size and entropy in [RFC 4086](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4086.html),
especially Sections 2 and 6.1.1.

## What counts as a collision?

A $b$-bit draw is a value from $0$ through $2^b-1$. Conversion to a
`ParticipantId` preserves that value without hashing or truncation.

```lean4 file="./Model.lean" declaration="toParticipantId"
```

The theorem `toParticipantId_eq_iff` proves that two converted IDs are equal
exactly when their sampled values are equal. A collision means that two
distinct generation positions produce equal IDs, regardless of where or when
they were generated.

```lean4 file="./Model.lean" declaration="HasCollision"
```

## The sampling model

An outcome is a function assigning one $b$-bit value to each of $n$ positions.
All $(2^b)^n$ outcomes have equal probability. This uniform product model
expresses independent, uniform sampling across every participant and location.
The probability of a collision is the number of colliding outcomes divided by
the number of all outcomes.

```lean4 file="./Model.lean" declaration="collisionProbability"
```

For example, two independent 2-bit draws have 16 equally likely outcomes.
Exactly four collide: $(0,0)$, $(1,1)$, $(2,2)$, and $(3,3)$. Their probability
is $4/16=1/4$. With three draws, 24 of the 64 outcomes have three different
values, so the collision probability is $40/64=5/8$. The executable tests
enumerate these small cases using the same definition as the proof.

## The bound

For each pair of distinct positions, fixing equal values removes one free
choice from the outcome. Its collision probability is at most $1/2^b$.
There are $\binom n2$ pairs. Adding their probabilities bounds the probability
of their union, even though one outcome can collide at several pairs:

$$
\Pr[\text{at least one collision}]
\leq \frac{\binom n2}{2^b}
= \frac{n(n-1)}{2^{b+1}}.
$$

The proof establishes a counting inequality before dividing by the total
number of outcomes. It does not assume the birthday bound or estimate the
probability by random testing.

```lean4 file="./Verification.lean" declaration="birthday_bound"
```

In the three-draw, 2-bit example, the bound is $3/4$, while the exact probability
is $5/8$. The bound can overcount collisions; it need not be an equality.

## A concrete requirement

Here $n$ counts **all generation attempts across the system**, including values
later discarded. The chosen requirement applies to at most $10^9$ attempts,
with a risk limit of $2^{-128}$.

```lean4 file="./Spec.lean" declaration="BillionIdRiskSpec"
```

For $b=256$ and $n=10^9$, the upper bound is approximately
$4.32\times10^{-60}$. Lean verifies the strict comparison to $2^{-128}$ using
exact arithmetic, and proves the requirement for every smaller number of draws.

## What the proof assumes

The distribution on outcomes is part of the model. Two devices that reuse the
same sampled value can each have a uniform marginal distribution while always
colliding; they do not satisfy the independent product model. Merely storing
256 bits does not establish 256 bits of independent randomness.

The proof does not verify an entropy source, an operating-system random API,
a pseudorandom generator, private-key generation, or derived wallet addresses.
The conversion above is executable but does not acquire randomness. No runtime
generator has yet been connected to this model, so the theorem does not assert
a probability bound for the current `register NATURAL_NUMBER` command. The
existing registration rule rejects an already recorded ID; that deterministic
property is separate from the probability that two generation attempts collide.
